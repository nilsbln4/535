Built Beats Building

Long existing AI capacity, short leveraged new build. Beta-neutral to QQQ.

Thesis

When new AI data centers get harder to build, the capacity that already exists becomes more valuable, and the companies that still have to build it, with borrowed money, lose.

In plain language

For two years the market paid for anyone who promised more AI computing capacity. The demand is still there. What has changed is the cost of meeting it. Towns are voting data centers down because they push up power bills and employ few people. States are pausing permits (New York) or making data centers pay for their own grid upgrades (Pennsylvania). Interest rates are staying high, so borrowed money costs more.

When building gets harder, a data center that is already built and connected to power is worth more. Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Equinix and Digital Realty already own that capacity and pay for growth out of revenue. CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN and Applied Digital are valued on capacity they have not built yet, funded with debt that costs up to 16%.

We buy the first group and short the second, sized so that an ordinary market move cancels out. The trade makes money if the gap between the two groups widens, whether AI stocks as a whole go up or down.

1Thesis

AI demand has not slowed. What has changed is the cost of adding capacity. Local voters, state moratoria and tariffs that make data centers pay for their own grid upgrades all raise the price of each new megawatt. Barclays calls it a shift from a race to a "war of attrition," where scarce power favours the largest balance sheets. Two groups come out of this differently:

Long · already built

Hyperscalers and data-center landlords with energized capacity. Capex is a fraction of revenue and the interest bill is small.

    Short · still building

    GPU clouds and developers whose growth depends on new sites funded with debt. Capex is several times revenue, and interest takes up to a quarter of it.

      The position is beta-neutral. The shorts move roughly four times as much as the longs with the Nasdaq-100 (beta about 2.9 against 0.7), so the short side is sized at about $0.25 per $1 long. That ratio is not fixed: it is re-measured from the last 60 sessions on the first trading day of each month. An equal-dollar version would really be a bet on AI stocks falling; on past data it lost 88%.

      Spread return = average return of the 5 longs − h × average return of the 4 shorts,  h = βlong ÷ βshort ≈ 0.25

      Example: QQQ falls 1%. The longs fall about 0.7% and the shorts about 2.9%, so the spread is −0.7% + 0.25 × 2.9% ≈ 0. A market move alone does nothing. The spread moves only when the two groups pull apart.

      The bigger picture: GDP, debt and tax revenue

      GDP

      The US needs growth to stay ahead of its debt, and the AI buildout is a large part of current growth. This thesis does not bet against that growth. It bets on who captures it: the companies that can keep building when building gets expensive.

      Debt

      Heavy federal borrowing helps keep rates higher for longer, and the buildout itself is increasingly debt-funded. CoreWeave's 2032 notes yield above 16%; interest already takes 23% of revenue at CoreWeave and Applied Digital. Equinix and Digital Realty carry debt too, so the long side has less rate risk but not none.

      Tax revenue

      Towns courted data centers for property tax. But data centers employ few people and push up local power bills: electricity prices are up 43% since 2021 against 28% for all prices. Voters decided the tax money was not worth it and rejected data centers in 40 of 41 local ballot measures in November.

      Predictions

      1. The spread is positive over 6 and 12 months from inception.
      2. It rises on news that restricts new data centers and falls on news that overrides local rules.
      3. Its return is not explained by the market: beta to QQQ stays near zero.

      Wrong if

      1. The spread falls 15% from its high since inception.
      2. The short basket beats the long basket by 25 points since inception.
      3. Federal rules override local and state data-center limits.
      4. GPU clouds refinance cheaply, for example high-yield notes well below 10%.

      4Catalysts to date

        "AI pushback is bipartisan but local, making the buildout pricier rather than stalling it. The AI race shifts from a prisoner's dilemma to a war of attrition: scarce power favors the biggest balance sheets."Dominique Toublan, head of US credit strategy, Barclays, note of 4 Nov 2026, the day after the midterms

        Context: until now each company had to build as fast as possible because its rivals were (the prisoner's dilemma). With power and permits as the constraint, the winner is whoever can last longest and pay most. The next session, the GPU clouds fell hardest: IREN −17%, Nebius −15%, CoreWeave −10%, against Microsoft −3.6% (CNBC).

        5Another way to implement it

        Shorting these stocks is expensive and risky. Borrow fees are high, and stocks with about 100% annualised volatility can jump sharply on a single headline. A bear call spread expresses the same view on the short side with a known maximum loss and no borrow fee.

        Bear call spread on each short name

        • Structure: sell a call about 10% above the stock price and buy a call about 30% above, both about five weeks out. Repeat each month.
        • Sizing: the spread's delta replaces the stock short. A 110/130 spread has a delta of about 0.20, so about 5 spreads give the same starting market exposure as shorting 1 share. Re-size at each roll, as the hedge ratio does.
        • Collateral: the maximum loss is held as margin, so the risk is known when the trade is opened.

        Better than shorting when

        1. The stocks stall rather than crash. The spread earns its full premium if the stock goes nowhere.
        2. A squeeze hits. Losses stop at a known amount instead of growing without limit.
        3. Borrow is expensive or unavailable.

        Worse when

        1. The thesis plays out fully and a stock collapses. The spread keeps only its premium.
        2. The stock rises 15–50%. At the same starting exposure, the spread loses more than the short stock in that range.
        3. Its exposure drifts as the stock moves, so it needs more re-sizing than a share short.

        The view behind the spread is slightly different: the shorts fail to rally, rather than the shorts fall. That fits the Barclays line, which describes a buildout made pricier, not stopped. The live tracker in Section 2 uses the stock short because it measures the thesis most directly.

        6Methodology

        Baskets
        Equal weight within each side, rebalanced daily. Price returns, before dividends.
        Hedge
        Short dollars per $1 long = beta of the long basket ÷ beta of the short basket, both against QQQ over the previous 60 sessions. Set at inception, then on the first session of each month, using only data from before that day.
        Not modelled
        Stock borrow fees, financing and commissions. Borrow on heavily shorted GPU-cloud stocks can be expensive, so the live spread overstates what a real short would earn.
        Data
        Prices: daily bars from the course server (Interactive Brokers), with LSEG and then Yahoo Finance as fallbacks (Yahoo matches LSEG closes to within 0.001%). Fundamentals: LSEG. Electricity and CPI: FRED. Only basket-level indices and ratios are published, never raw vendor prices.
        Pipeline
        The course server pushes daily bars to this repository. A GitHub Action recomputes the live spread after every push and every weekday evening, then commits the result. This page reads it.
